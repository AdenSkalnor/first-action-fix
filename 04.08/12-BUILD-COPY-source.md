## PAGE 2 — Why educational email works
‹education page; question numbers below are structure only — do not render them›

`[Box 1 — what it is]`
COPY: A free five-day email course that teaches a problem the buyer already has, and ends at the product.

`[Box 2 — who]`
COPY: For construction software where signups arrive and most never reach a second session.

`[Box 3 — what changes]`
COPY: A visitor who would have left leaves an address instead — and comes back taught.

`[Box 4 — why it works + table]`
COPY: Buyers compare across thousands of vendors, jumping tab to tab. Winning that attention with genuine advice — and a contact — is a large gain over a visitor who just left and costs ad rates to reach again. A landing page keeps 2–5%; the course keeps more of the rest, and a known sender reads as safe.

`[Box 4b — table, per 10,000 unique visits]`

| Take the primary door | 200–500 | 200–500 |

| Addresses kept | 0 | ~200 |

`[Box 5 — the catch]`
COPY: Below ~2,000 sessions a month it recovers a rounding error, and around 400 it is about one conversion a quarter — thin enough that I would usually suggest waiting, and not a case for buying clicks to feed it.

## PAGE 3 — Why behavioral email works
‹education page; question numbers are structure only›

`[Box 1 — what it is]`
COPY: Email that fires on a customer's action — abandoned signup, quiet trial, upgrade page opened and closed, failed card — instead of on a date.

`[Box 2 — who]`
COPY: For a trial with enough signups that individual moments go unanswered.

`[Box 3 — what changes]`
COPY: The message arrives at the moment of a decision instead of on a schedule.

`[Box 4 — why it works, prose]`
COPY: The message competes for a crowded inbox: the average worker takes ~120 emails a day, and the company behind the trial runs ~11 separate software tools, each sending its own nudges. The difference between a message that reads "they noticed me" and one that reads "a statistic I didn't need" is the difference between a habit of trust and a habit of ignoring — both compound.

COPY: Calendar mail hits the week; a trigger hits the moment. With no mail, 3–5 of every 100 trials pay. Calendar drip lifts that a couple of points, to about 5–7. Behavioral, set up modestly, adds roughly one more, to 6–8 — the gain comes from landing at the moment of a decision rather than on a schedule.

COPY: The plumbing case computes plainest. Say 100 customers cancel in a year. 20–40 of those are failed cards, not decisions — people who meant to stay. Left alone, about 6–12 come back. With retry logic and card updaters, 11–28 do — roughly 5–16 accounts saved, for the least writing of anything here.

`[Box 5 — the catch]`
COPY: It works best where the events already exist — reaching the sender, carrying an identity, with ~90 days of history behind them. Where they do not, drip is usually the better place to start, the wiring named as the gap. Under ~50 signups a month, a founder often does better writing personally.
